University

I started at McGill back in 2021 at the tail end of the COVID-19 pandemic. Entering in Bioengineering, I had no clue what I was doing. I was lucky enough to receive a Prestige Scholarship (the JW McConnell Award), making the finances of university less strenuous than for others, though for the first time I was no longer the big fish in a small pond. Suddenly, direction was something I seemed to lack compared to my many private school colleauges with substantial preparedness and direction from parents, school, and their community. Growing up in a small town has some benefits, but an inherent "push" from your community for greatness is generally not one: it has to be derived from within. After one egregious calculus midterm grade, I was set in motion.

My first successful ELISpot assay against some HIV-infected T cells stimulated with different antigens.


After turning around my calculus performance and ending my first year with a perfect 4.0 GPA, I found passion for disease research at Ostrowski Lab at U of T. At the time, I was especially interested in viruses and thought I'd pursue an HIV project with Ostrowski Lab. Getting my first laboratory training and contributing significantly to a project that would be eventually published in Cell gave me a taste for something I came to obsess over. Furthermore, I was able to see real-world impact for people with HIV; our group discovered that three doses of all mainstream SARS-CoV-2 vaccines offered in Canada had insignificant impact on the stability of the HIV reservoir. In other words, COVID-19 vaccines were safe for people with HIV to take; this would drastically lower their risk for developing opportunistic infections during the pandemic.

Our iGEM-winning CRISPR-Cas protease; the system recognizes an RNA sequence, then cleaves a sequence in the Csx30 protein, which we synthetically inserted within the linker region of human gasdermin D as a therapeutic capable of killing cancer cells.


Returning to McGill in the fall, I needed to find a way to satisfy my new love for biomedical research. I sat in at the Synbio Canada conference and heard a talk from Prof. Noémie Dorval about a new biomaterial that her lab had developed that allowed ripped clothing to be fixed simply by adding water! They called it self-healing clothing; it was made using bioengineered E. coli curli fibres, and purportedly had dozens of other applications of the modified fibres. As soon as I could, I reached out to Prof. Dorval and began helping out on a project there funded by Lululemon (after all, clothing companies are particularly interested in this sort of technology). I would continue working here for a few more years after. My primary work was focused on pH- and temperature-sensitive biomaterials. I was obsessed with the project before we finally published in ACS Synbio and I loved continuing to develop my skills as a researcher.

Our cartoon graphical abstract of a dual pH- temperature-responsive biosensor for applications in clothing, disease monitoring, disease detection, and living materials.


In my same year back at McGill, I ran into two friends that had been working tirelessly towards a club called iGEM and were encouraging me to join. After ideating on a project together, we all decided we'd make it our mission to combat one of the deadliest cancers in the world: pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. After many 80-hour weeks throughout the following summer and perhaps the largest project I've ever co-managed, our group was seeing positive results of our therapeutic in our initial eukaryotic chassis (S. cerevisiae), we had raised $150,000 towards our project (basically to the scale of a seed round for some startups), and we had sent 14 McGill students (including ourselves) to Paris for the iGEM International Competition to present our work and results. We ended up winning the competition among over 350 competing universities (including teams from schools like Harvard, Yale, Stanford, MIT, etc.). Talking to PDAC survivors and patients was a devastating experience, and huge strides are necessary to improve survival rates for this horrifying disease. I hope our work inspires future efforts into combatting it. See my team's presentation on the big stage in Paris in front of 5000 audience members, where we discuss how we aimed to fight PDAC:

After the iGEM high, I needed to find my next fix. I'd become really interested in entrepreneurship following developing Proteus with my iGEM team, leading me to consider building my own startup. After going to some biotech entrepreneurship events in Montreal and speaking with professors at McGill, I got in touch with Nomic Bio. When I first heard of them, they were a fledgling startup that had recently raised their Series A to acquire their first clients and further validate their product. Nomic's mission was one of the most inspiring stories for me; both of the cofounders had personal family experience with cancer, making them the most dedicated, driven advocates for advancing early diagnosis efforts for cancer. Both believe that solving cancer will not come down to some miracle drug (after all, cancer is highly diverse; no one-size-fits-all solution will ever be effective), but rather improving diagnosis so that it can be treated earlier on before metastasis. This perspective really resonated with me, and I immediately saw the potential with the idea. I was able to convince the cofounders to hire me full time as their youngest ever employee. I got to work on Nomic's most important obstacle: improving sensitivity. While the high plex of Nomic's assay allows it to provide unmatched insights on cell phenotype and disease state, without improving sensitivity, many low-concentration indicators of disease may go unnoticed. While I was offered to stay on permanently (which would mean dropping out of undergrad), I made the difficult decision to return to my studies. Not without leaving a permanent impact on Nomic's efforts to improve its sensitivity, however! Today, Nomic has gone on to raise $42 million in a Series B and has more than doubled in size since my time there. I can't wait to see the progress they make on detecting and curing disease before it takes hold.

Nomic calls themselves the Protein Profiling Company. With assays now reaching over 1000-plex, Nomic is unmatched in the high-plex proteomics space. Their technology will provide essential proteome-wide insights on disease indicators and early-stage detection.


It was especially hard to return to my studies after Nomic; I finally felt like I was working on real-world, impactful projects in the space I love: biotech. Though sunk-cost fallacy kicked in and I continued my studies. However, for a few years now I had been aware of the Institute of Protein Design out at University of Washington and I had become really interested in de novo protein design as an effect of some of the more recent work I was doing at Dorval Lab. Thus, I reached out to some grad students at Baker Lab and secured an internship down there for the following summer. During that time, David Baker was awarded the 2024 Chemistry Nobel Laureate for his work on de novo design, and I was so excited to have the opportunity to work in such a revolutionary laboratory.

A de novo-designed AB42 protease that I designed using the breakthrough machine-learning methods developed in Baker Lab. The red linear substrate is a truncated AB42 and the purple protein is the engineered protease with its catalytic residues exposed.


After a highly productive summer out in Seattle, I had successfully designed de novo amyloid beta-42 (AB42) proteases as a potential therapeutic against Alzheimer's. Currently, I'm continuing work on designing other proteases binding other amyloidogenic fibrils with researchers in Baker Lab; if we see positive results, we're hoping to publish work on our design process and the potential therapeutic impact of de novo amyloid proteases for halting disease progression.

A graphical abstract of my work at Baker Lab.


During my time at Baker Lab, I was helping out part time with a student-led VC firm called Front Row Ventures (FRV). While I really enjoyed what I learned about the world of alternative, high-risk investing, I think I came to the conclusion that FRV was not for me and promptly ended that stint. Writing highly repetitive 2-pagers and due diligence reports, then meeting in groups to discuss our "ratings" of early-stage firms' ideas felt rather monotonous and ineffective for driving real change. At this point, I think I realized that I much prefer being on the inventive side of entrepreneurship rather than the provider-of-capital side. Regardless, I really appreciated what I was able to learn about the investment ecosystem from my colleagues at FRV and will take this learning forward if I go on to seek funding for an idea of my own.
