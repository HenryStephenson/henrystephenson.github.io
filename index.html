⮞ Dorval Laboratory

During my time at the Dorval Lab, I was primarily working with Dalia Saldanha on heat- and pH-sensitive biomaterials for biocompatible wearable sensor applications. My work culminated in a second authorship on Saldanha et al., ACS Synthetic Biology.

For this publication, Dalia and I developed engineered curli fibres (extracellular E. coli-based protein fibres) to be pH sensitive using pHuji fluorescent protein. We also took advantage of E. coli's native pHS promoter, a heat shock promoter that is specifically induced between 37C to 43C, to produce another fluorescent reporter in the presence of beyond-homeostatic temperatures (which might be an indicator of infection or cancer in a local area). We fundamentally created whole-cell temperature sensors that could be embedded into the pH-sensitive curli fibre matrix for dual temperature and pH monitoring using living sensors and biomaterials. This work paves the way for smart, living, bioresponsive textiles

Cartoon graphical abstract of dual responsive biosensor.

The project was partially funded by Lululemon, because they were interested initially in developing temperature-sensitive biocompatible textiles which might be useful for clothing that naturally releases pleasant odours when skin temperature or pH changes (maybe indicative of a hard workout or sweat). While our project eventually led to more biomedical applications, this is still an interesting potential application of the work. However, the medical applications may be more promising. After all, wearable, minimally invasive, biocompatible dual sensors for pH and temperature are essential for monitoring patients or could be applied for rapid diagnosis of disease. Our work opens the door for genetically customizable, multifunctional biomaterial sensors.

⮞ Ostrowski Laboratory

I worked with the Ostrowski Lab on a study that evaluated the effects of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination on the HIV reservoir. A manuscript describing the work is available (see the lab link) and I performed ELISpot assays that measured cytokine responses — specifically interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukin-2 (IL-2) — from PBMCs. These ELISpot readouts were used to quantify antigen-specific cellular responses and assess whether vaccination transiently perturbed markers of HIV persistence.

My laboratory work involved preparing PBMC samples, running and optimizing ELISpot conditions, counting and validating spot-forming units, and compiling the experimental data used in the immunological figures. These data helped show that COVID-19 vaccination does not meaningfully disrupt the HIV reservoir, providing important reassurance that vaccination is safe for people living with HIV (PLWH).

⮞ Baker Lab (David Baker) — Protease design against amyloid

At the Baker Lab in Seattle I worked on a protein design workflow aimed at engineering proteases to target amyloidogenic proteins implicated in neurodegenerative disease. The design pipeline I used typically moves from generative backbone construction → sequence recovery → in silico filtering → structural validation:

  1. RFDiffusion / generative backbone design — building candidate backbone geometries compatible with the desired active site or binding interface.
  2. ProteinMPNN — running sequence design to propose amino-acid sequences that fold to the designed backbone.
  3. AlphaFold (AlphaFold3 / AF2/AF3) — structure prediction to validate whether the designed sequence actually folds to the target conformation and to assess confidence metrics (pLDDT, interface RMSD, etc.).
  4. Filtering — selecting top designs based on predicted stability, interface metrics, and retained catalytic geometry for proteolytic activity.

My contributions included running design jobs, filtering and ranking candidates, and creating visualizations (PyMOL snapshots) of top designs. I also helped prepare sequences for downstream experimental testing and annotated designs for ease of reproduction.
