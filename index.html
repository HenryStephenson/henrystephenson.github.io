⮞ Dorval Laboratory

During my time at the Dorval Lab, I was primarily working with Dalia Saldanha on heat- and pH-sensitive biomaterials for biocompatible wearable sensor applications. My work culminated in a second authorship on Saldanha et al., ACS Synthetic Biology.

For this publication, Dalia and I developed engineered curli fibres (extracellular E. coli-based protein fibres) to be pH sensitive using pHuji fluorescent protein. We also took advantage of E. coli's native pHS promoter, a heat shock promoter that is specifically induced between 37C to 43C, to produce another fluorescent reporter in the presence of beyond-homeostatic temperatures (which might be an indicator of infection or cancer in a local area). We fundamentally created whole-cell temperature sensors that could be embedded into the pH-sensitive curli fibre matrix for dual temperature and pH monitoring using living sensors and biomaterials. This work paves the way for smart, living, bioresponsive textiles

Cartoon graphical abstract of dual responsive biosensor.


The project was partially funded by Lululemon, because they were interested initially in developing temperature-sensitive biocompatible textiles which might be useful for clothing that naturally releases pleasant odours when skin temperature or pH changes (maybe indicative of a hard workout or sweat). While our project eventually led to more biomedical applications, this is still an interesting potential application of the work. However, the medical applications may be more promising. After all, wearable, minimally invasive, biocompatible dual sensors for pH and temperature are essential for monitoring patients or could be applied for rapid diagnosis of disease. Our work opens the door for genetically customizable, multifunctional biomaterial sensors.

⮞ Ostrowski Laboratory

In first year summer of undergrad, I was working down at U of T with medical specialist Mario Ostrowski. Dr. Ostrowski completed his MD at U of T then went on to research HIV for over 30 years. At the time, I was fascinated by viral infections and began helping on a project exploring the effects of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination on the stability of the HIV reservoir; basically, would COVID-19 vaccines be safe for people with HIV, or would they cause HIV to reactivate? I also supported on a project working with Hepatitis C, another incurable disease...

{% include embedpdf.html file="../assets/other/ostrowski_paper.pdf" %}

For the HIV paper published in Cell, I was primarily in charge of measuring patient-derived PBMC (peripheral blood mononuclear cell) responses to stimulation with SARS-CoV-2 antigen HIV proteins and controls. The idea was to measure T cell response to SARS-CoV-2 for people living with HIV (PLWH) before and after vaccination to determine if vaccination is still beneficial for people with immunodeficiency virus. We also stimulated with HIV antigen to see if vaccination would have deleterious effects on the stability of the HIV reservoir. This involved measuring interferon gamma (IFNγ) and interleukin 2 (IL-2) responses, both pro-inflammatory cytokines, using ELISpot, a more quantitative ELISA in a sense.

I collected fundamentally all data for this figure and wrote the relevant paragraphs for this section of the publication. The goal of this figure is to establish that immune responders (IRs) indeed see a positive impact on their immune response to SARS-CoV-2, demonstrating the efficacy of vaccination.


While the initial plan was just to have me work on the T cell SARS-CoV-2 and HIV responses, I excelled in my role and was able to take on additional responsibility; This involved performing IPDAs (intact proviral DNA assays) to further validate the stability of the HIV reservoir post-vaccination. Luckily, with these assays, we also found that HIV genes were not upregulated with as many as three doses of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, supporting the argument that vaccines were safe for PLWH (at least those not classified as PLWH with unsuppressed viremia).

The data I collected for this figure basically supports that the vast majority of PLWH did not see significant increases in intact HIV proviruses in their T cells post-vaccination, supporting that vaccination is safe for PLWH!


All in all, it was really exciting to play a critical part in an investigation that had real-world impact on the health and safety of those suffering from HIV. The results of our study basically confirmed that vaccination would be safe for PLWH, drastically reducing the risk of life-threatening COVID-19 as a result of opportunistic infection in people living with HIV. This was my first real-world experience where I realized how research directly leads to tangible change. This would be the first of many moments throughout my undergrad career.

⮞ Nomic Bio — Assay Engineering & R&D Intern

Nomic Bio builds high-throughput, highly multiplexed proteomic solutions (the “Omni 1000” and nELISA technologies) designed to democratize proteomics by making broad protein profiling fast, sensitive and affordable. During my internship on the Assay Engineering and R&D team I was the youngest employee hired at the time and I worked on improving assay sensitivity and robustness so the platform could detect biologically relevant signatures and biomarkers across diverse disease contexts.

My work focused on assay optimization (sample handling, normalization strategies, and tuning assay conditions) and on downstream analysis pipelines that help turn high-dimensional protein measurements into actionable insights for diagnosis and biomarker discovery. The improvements I contributed to helped the team better capture subtle disease-related signals and improved the platform’s ability to inform biomarker hypotheses.

⮞ Baker Lab (IPD) — Protease Design Against Amyloid Fibres

At the Baker Lab in Seattle I worked on a protein design workflow aimed at engineering proteases to target amyloidogenic proteins implicated in neurodegenerative disease. The design pipeline I used typically moves from generative backbone construction → sequence recovery → in silico filtering → structural validation:

  1. RFDiffusion / generative backbone design — building candidate backbone geometries compatible with the desired active site or binding interface.
  2. ProteinMPNN — running sequence design to propose amino-acid sequences that fold to the designed backbone.
  3. AlphaFold (AlphaFold3 / AF2/AF3) — structure prediction to validate whether the designed sequence actually folds to the target conformation and to assess confidence metrics (pLDDT, interface RMSD, etc.).
  4. Filtering — selecting top designs based on predicted stability, interface metrics, and retained catalytic geometry for proteolytic activity.

My contributions included running design jobs, filtering and ranking candidates, and creating visualizations (PyMOL snapshots) of top designs. I also helped prepare sequences for downstream experimental testing and annotated designs for ease of reproduction.
